### E.23:4 - Solution

`E.23` guides repeated improvement of one object version under a settled evaluation question. Reuse an adequate ordinary request; use the E.22 `QualityEvaluationQuestionFrame` and `QualityEvaluationUseDeclaration` when a receiving use needs those exact declarations. The evaluation pattern defines the evaluation; restoration and subject patterns supply the relevant rules or guidance. A pattern body or locator does not by itself establish a semantic `U.Method` or qualify as its description. Claim Method or MethodDescription identity only after A.3.1 and A.3.2 admit it.

When an evaluation or improvement pass is claimed as actual A.15.1 `U.Work`, recover every exact actual performer through A.13 and independently identify the occurrence, time, Method, and containing System through A.15.1. Add A.2.1 assignment-occurrence identity and F.6 only when the loop record or receiving use expressly represents precise assignment-bound attribution through the same obtaining A.13 assignment; missing or failed attribution alone leaves independent Work admission intact. If the same finding invalidates a premise consumed by A.13 or A.15.1, apply A.15.1's premise-recheck rule to that premise and its dependent conclusions, preserving independently grounded history and relations. A proposed pass, intended performer, planned condition, or A.15.2 WorkPlan remains modal until its predicates obtain.

Keep a returned value, durable result episteme, changed object and actual Transformation separate. Connect a returned value through its A.6.1 binding or the evaluation’s direct result relation. Connect Work to a result or change only through a declared direct relation or supported local claim that actually obtains. Return `missing-governor` when the required predicate or governing condition is unavailable. If the governor exists, apply its criterion to the case: retain a supported positive, negative or inapplicable result, or name the missing facts and how they can be obtained.

The repeated organization changes the object, re-evaluates the changed version through the same declared method and quality model, checks trade-offs and cost, and exposes admissible stop, continue, switch, new-frame, information-hold, branch, and subject-pattern-return continuations. When the receiving use needs its structure, admit that organization under A.22.CGUS through §4.2a; use E.18 only when an independently selected transformation-flow structure is actually the EntityOfConcern. Neither the method, record, visible cycle, nor selected continuation is an enduring Work occurrence or context container.

#### E.23:4.1 - Ordinary loop method

For one quality-improvement loop:

1. Name the exact object version and the evaluation that will judge it. Reuse the current `QualityEvaluationQuestionFrame` and `QualityEvaluationUseDeclaration` when they still fit the purpose and receiving use. Keep the evaluator, evaluation pattern or Method, characteristic space, evidence basis, result form, ClaimScope, and qualification window separate.
2. State the content change sought, protected trade-offs, cost and risk account, and local stop condition. Do not use `5`, all-`5`, or `5-defensible` as the target; say what should become better in practice.
3. Resolve any remaining ambiguity in purpose, scope or result-consuming work or decision through E.22. Keep an adequate question; constitute a separate E.22 frame only when its exact declaration is needed.
4. Run the declared evaluation. When E.21 is the selected evaluation, retain its complete result: every coordinate, `ShortRationale`, `PrecisionRestorationProfile`, evidence basis, coordinate payload, and status. A loop note, blocker summary, or "no blockers" statement is not a substitute. If dated evaluation Work is asserted, identify it and its result binding or direct result relation; keep any durable result episteme separate.
5. Record each returned finding or proposal separately. A grouped memory summary does not close skipped items, and a proposal remains a proposed next action rather than performed Work.
6. Select the next change. Selection does not perform it. When the account asserts dated improvement Work, identify that Work and connect it to a returned value or changed object only through an obtaining A.6.1 binding or declared Work-to-result or Work-to-change relation. If that relation is unavailable, keep proposal, Work, changed object, and Transformation separate and return the missing relation.

   Repair below-floor findings first. Above the floor, prefer a substantive gain—such as clearer action, a missing case or countercase, current source support, restored predecessor content, cleaner relations, or a split of overloaded material. Do not add guards, catalogues, or quality proof merely to defend a higher score. Close with no change only after the evidence shows that no feasible non-dominated improvement remains worth its cost under the protected trade-offs.

   For a precision-restoration defect, apply `F.19` and open a restoration or subject pattern for an unresolved FPF-specific meaning. Claim a Method or MethodDescription only when A.3.1 and A.3.2 admit it. Keep locator, Method, description, performer, assignment, Work, result, and responsibility separate. Run one bounded `KindRestorationCheck` when the changed expression can alter FPF-governed meaning; otherwise F.19's local revalidation completes the ordinary repair.
7. Re-evaluate the changed object as a separate pass through the same declared evaluation with comparable evidence and conditions. If the evaluation itself needs to change, use §4.1a before comparing values across that change. Keep the later Work, application or direct result relation, evidence, returned value, and result episteme distinct from the first pass.
8. Record what improved, what stayed at the floor, what was unchanged by value, what became worse, and which findings moved outside this evaluation. Compare the earlier and later evaluation results. When result epistemes or dated Work are asserted, preserve their separately governed identities.
9. Decide `stop`, `continue`, `switchMethodFamily`, `openNewFrame`, or `holdUntilInformationBasisSufficient`. When later replay depends on alternatives and guards, use the conditional structure block below. A decision or selected continuation neither authorizes nor performs the next Work.
10. Leave an account that lets the next reader recover the object versions, evaluation, proposals, performed passes, result or change bases, evidence, trade-offs, cost and risk, continuation, stop and return boundaries, and the reason for the decision. Use the structured `QualityImprovementLoopRecord` only when a named replay, handoff, audit, or machine-facing use needs that form; otherwise a short result with the same recoverable facts is enough.

Stop here when this route answers the current use. Open the names, record schemas, and unfolding-structure block below only when a named receiving use depends on that added assurance detail.

##### E.23:4.1.1 - When evaluation can imitate improvement

Use this branch when sampling, repeated assessment, selection or the calculation of a reported result could produce the apparent gain while the claimed change is absent. The practical question is not just how variable the score is, but what this particular procedure would report without that change. Keep an existing comparison when it already answers that question adequately.

**Name the claim and the reported quantity.** Better average performance, newly successful cases, fewer losses and faster subsequent learning are different quantities. State the object, cases, conditions and change that the comparison concerns. A control for one quantity cannot silently support another: a stable average does not prevent cases from moving in both directions between success and failure.

**Construct the absence of the claimed change.** Use an applicable model, a matched control, or both. For an unchanged stochastic solver, the model can describe repeated attempts under unchanged conditions. For a grading procedure, reassess the same archived answers without revealing their earlier grades. Say what remains fixed and what can vary. If a changed task population, assistance or operating condition can explain the difference, include it in the comparison or narrow the claim. Use `C.16.MR` to construct the measurement relation, `C.16.IR` to determine what it can resolve, and `C.16.RM` when the arrangement needs repair; the relevant mathematical or empirical method supplies the particular calculation or study.

**Carry that absence through the whole procedure.** Apply the same inclusion rule, thresholds, selection of a best attempt or version, recording, calculation and stopping rule used for the reported gain. If only initially unsuccessful cases are counted, reproduce that conditioning too. Preserve shared cases and other dependence: all ordered pairs of a few repeated runs are not that many independent experiments. An idealized model is sufficient only where its assumptions support the intended use; an empirical control can expose effects omitted from it, such as altered batching or assessor behavior.

**Use the resulting behavior to decide the stated question.** Obtain the relevant distribution or control estimate and its uncertainty, then choose a comparison rule for the intended claim and tolerated error. A mean control value is not itself a threshold, and subtracting it is not a universal correction. Check a rule tuned on control results on material not used for that tuning. Carry the uncertainty of the control into the final comparison. If the arrangement cannot distinguish a worthwhile change from these effects, obtain more informative evidence, improve the arrangement or leave the claim unresolved. Failure to detect a difference does not establish equivalence or absence of improvement. A difference beyond the control still needs any additional evidence required by a causal or wider capability claim.

**Worked calculation.** Suppose an unchanged solver has probability 1/2 of success on each of 20 cases, with independent attempts and independent cases. In two evaluations, failure followed by success has probability `(1/2) × (1/2) = 1/4`; success followed by failure has the same probability. The expected counts are five new successes and five losses, while expected net change is zero. The new-success count has distribution `Binomial(20, 1/4)`: the probability of eight or more is about `0.1018`, and of nine or more about `0.04093`. For a single comparison whose rule was fixed in advance, nine would therefore meet a chosen 5% upper-tail rule under this particular model; eight would not. Choosing the best of several comparisons changes that calculation. Neither threshold establishes a cause, a general capability or improvement on a different task population. These are calculated properties of the constructed example, not intervention data.

**Human and organizational limits.** Regrading unchanged answers tests variation in assessment, not whether their author learned. Reprocessing unchanged service records tests the reporting procedure, not whether the service improved. A person can retain practice, fatigue or changed expectations, and an organization retains its history even if a document is restored. Construct a comparison appropriate to the claimed change in their work and support; do not manufacture an unchanged person or organization by copying an instruction. If only the reporting artifact can be held fixed, preserve that useful bounded result and leave the stronger claim open.

#### E.23:4.1a - When the evaluation needs to change

Use this branch when a missed defect, false objection, harmful proposed repair, changed use or excessive evaluation cost gives reason to reconsider the evaluation. An ordinary object repair can keep using the existing adequate evaluation.

First identify what changed. A new object version, a new requirement, a different task population, a different measuring procedure and a different evaluator implementation can require different responses. Fixing an implementation error can preserve the intended evaluation while invalidating the results affected by that error. A threshold that varies with load under a fixed rule still uses that rule; it does not by itself change the evaluation.

Keep the evaluation meanings and comparison conditions stable within one comparison. To change them between cycles, use `A.19.ECS:4.5`: compare the old and proposed evaluations on independently grounded defect and admissible cases, inspect harmful repairs and full cost, and use a new independent case when known cases were used for tuning. Decide whether to adopt the change for a stated use, retain it on trial, revise it, reject it or keep the current evaluation. A stricter result or higher evaluator agreement is not the adoption criterion.

After adoption, name which earlier conclusions depend on the changed rule. Re-evaluate affected preserved cases where cross-evaluation comparison is needed; otherwise retain earlier results with their original scope. Declare a bridge when it is justified, and leave unlike values incomparable when it is not. New task populations and requirements create new questions without retroactively falsifying supported old answers.

**Recover how the evidence was obtained.** The old criterion may have decided which variants to generate, which cases to examine, which branches to continue and which observations to retain, as well as how to score an answer. Changing a score does not undo these earlier choices. Start from the receiving decision under the adopted evaluation and trace the evidence it actually uses. For each affected result, recover the object or response, case and conditions, observation or raw material, rule used for its assessment, and any old-criterion dependence in its generation, selection or retention. Use existing provenance when sufficient; add only the distinctions needed to settle the present use.

Distinguish three kinds of repair. When the same intended evaluation had an implementation error and the saved observations contain the required information, correct the affected calculation and its dependents. When a changed evaluation can be applied to a preserved object, obtain the new judgement and retain the old one under its original question; evaluating an old artifact now is not rerunning its former performance. When the new question needs outcomes never observed, a different population or consequential conditions absent from the record, rescoring cannot provide them. Obtain suitable new evidence, use a justified inference under the relevant subject Method, or restrict the conclusion. A large archive can still lack precisely the needed observation.

**Rebuild the basis that directs the next choice.** Follow the affected results into the summaries and estimates used to choose further work: success proportions, branch or method rankings, accumulated utility, fitted predictors, uncertainty estimates and resource priorities. Suspend their affected use until their basis has been repaired. Do not mix old- and new-criterion values in one current estimate just because their field names agree. Recompute the needed dependents from still-applicable evidence and newly obtained judgements, keeping population, denominator, conditions and uncertainty meaningful. A narrow correction requires only the dependent part; independent observations and unrelated conclusions remain usable.

Keep raw artifacts, observations and earlier qualified results recoverable. Withdrawing an old contribution from a current utility estimate is not deleting the historical event or declaring it false. Where a record contains both an unaffected observation and a criterion-dependent judgement, separate their uses instead of discarding both. Conversely, retaining an artifact does not qualify an old ranking built from it. If dependency cannot be recovered, mark that basis unresolved for the affected decision rather than assuming independence.

The repaired result should let the practitioner make the next warranted choice: continue with a valid comparison, test a promising alternative under the new conditions, retain a limited old answer, or return an explicit unresolved question. Include the work of obtaining new evidence and rebuilding estimates in the decision. No automatic instruction to reevaluate the whole archive follows. C.40:4.8.1 supplies a bounded history replay when its recorded responses and conditions suffice; changing the evaluator may require repairing both those responses and the selection mechanism that chose them.

**Worked selection change.** An organization received ten project proposals. A low-upfront-cost criterion admitted six to pilots; four were never piloted. Complete pilot records permit a later, adopted reliability evaluation of the six: three satisfy it. Those records support the descriptive result “three of the six selected pilots meet the new criterion.” They do not support a reliability rate for all ten proposals, and the four absent outcomes are not four failures. The old cost criterion affected the population observed, not only the score assigned afterward.

The organization keeps all proposals and pilot observations, preserves the earlier cost judgements under their old use, and replaces the affected reliability judgements for the six. It withdraws an old all-project promise or resource priority if that claim depended on treating the selected pilots as representative. It then rebuilds the particular comparison needed for the next allocation. If the four untried designs are serious alternatives, an appropriate new pilot or justified subject model supplies their missing consequences; if they are irrelevant to the present decision, it can retain a narrower answer without testing them all. If the new requirement concerns a longer service period than any pilot recorded, even the six lack the required observation. A revised score cannot extend observation time.

**Human self-development.** A person formerly chose familiar exercises because the self-assessment rewarded fluent completion. A new question asks whether the acquired way works on unfamiliar problems. Saved answers can reveal previously missed reasoning defects, but awarding them new marks supplies no performance on the excluded problem classes. Preserve what those answers show, retire a transfer claim based only on the familiar exercises, and obtain suitable new cases. Update the practice-selection rule that used the old success estimate. Neither erasing the person's history nor averaging old fluency marks with new transfer results answers that question; the subsequent practice and its actual use remain separate from changing the evaluation.

**Worked slice.** A review evaluation rewards concise explanations but misses an omitted dependency that makes the instruction unusable. Adding the missing dependency improves the object under its existing usefulness requirement. Changing the evaluator so it notices that defect is a different proposal. Compare that evaluator with its predecessor on the defective text and a concise, fully usable alternative; reject a repair that makes every explanation longer without need. A new independently grounded case supports adoption beyond those development cases. Previously supported readability judgements remain scoped to their text and reading conditions; only conclusions depending on the missed dependency need reopening.

#### E.23:4.2 - Conditional names and kind settlement

Source and practitioner phrases such as "loop engineering", "agent loop", "harness loop", "prompt loop", and "workflow hardening loop" are entry phrases. Lower them into `ObjectUnderImprovementRef`, `QualityEvaluationQuestionFrame`, `QualityEvaluationUseDeclaration`, `ImprovementAim`, `MethodFamilySelection`, `CostAndRiskAccount`, and `QualityImprovementLoopRecord`, or else name the subject pattern for the live claim and leave `E.23` closed.

The two named forms organize claims within the claim content of a result or loop episteme; a table row or serialization may publish them but does not become the claim.

A `SourceComposedResultClaim` uses exactly one of `resultClaimNodeRef` and `resultClaimEpistemeRef`. `resultClaimNodeRef` is the C.2.1:4.2.5 `ClaimAddress` pair: an exact episteme-edition reference and an intrinsic claim identity declared by that edition's ClaimGraph. Resolve the edition and then require exactly one claim with that identity. An unresolved edition, absent or non-unique identity, or display-only node token leaves the address unresolved. An edition change requires an explicit claim correspondence for any address migration. When no stable intrinsic claim identity exists, use `resultClaimEpistemeRef` if the receiving use can take the whole episteme's content; otherwise constitute the required result claim as its own C.2.1 episteme and reference that whole episteme. A row number or rendering label does not supply the missing claim identity.

Checked evidence values stay paired with their kinds; evidence relations form a separate pair. Every actual Work reference points to an independently identified occurrence governed by the central §4 Work rule. A compact rendering may use only the omission allowed there.

For an improvement pass, the changed-version reference appears only after that version exists. The `workResultOrChange...` fields keep the declared predicate, its pattern locator, and the obtaining basis distinct. That basis must be an A.6.1 result binding, a direct Work-to-result or Work-to-change relation, or a local relation claim that names its participants, conditions, and obtaining facts. If only the predicate or locator is known, keep the proposal, Work, changed object and Transformation separate. Recover the predicate’s applicable test and the facts it needs. An unavailable definition or condition is `missing-governor`; unavailable occurrence evidence leaves the relation unestablished and returns that exact missing basis. A supported negative or inapplicable result retains its own meaning. An A.15.PROD route points to its applicable local claim rather than to the pattern as a generic claim.
The two record epistemes follow C.2.1 identity: claim content, exact EntityOfConcern, and effective reference scheme determine each episteme edition. The listed loop fields contribute to claim content; `editionId` designates an already distinguished edition but does not constitute it. Empirical grounding, viewpoint membership, claim scope, model-use structure, applicability, qualification, evidence currentness, and source currentness remain separate relations or values defined elsewhere. A change in one of them changes a record episteme only when its claim content, EntityOfConcern, or reference scheme is revised; carrier and support serialization alone change neither episteme. These records do not create quality values, project evidence, release state, selected-set result declaration, actual publication, parity, refresh, Work, Transformation, or proof of quality.

The `@Context` suffixes on support species such as `LoopEvaluationEvidenceBasis@Context`, `CandidateImprovementProposalRow@Context`, `TradeoffProtectionSet@Context`, and `ImprovementLoopBoundaryCondition@Context` are compatibility and retrieval spellings only. No suffix or context label supplies a container, participant, ClaimScope, applicability, or identity discriminator. Use the suffixless interface names `QualityEvaluationQuestionFrame`, `QualityEvaluationUseDeclaration`, and `QualityImprovementLoopRecord`.

#### E.23:4.2a - Conditional Improvement Unfolding Structure Block

Use this block when a named review or replay use relies on the improvement loop's constraint-governed unfolding structure rather than only its method record. It keeps the proposal epistemes, predicted evaluation-result changes, independently identified pass Work and results, guarded alternatives, decision value, information-basis hold, stop, and neighboring returns exact instead of treating them as generic structural locations.

```text
ImprovementUnfoldingStructureBlock:
  unfoldingStructureRef: U.EntityRef, referencing one ImprovementLoopUnfoldingStructure
```

#### E.23:4.3 - Stop, continue, and reopen

Stop when the current object version meets the declared floor or improvement aim and no feasible non-dominated proposal remains worth its cost under the current use, comparison set, source state, and protected trade-offs. If the remaining proposal mainly makes a value easier to argue while adding apparatus or worsening use, affordability, locality, source preservation, or ecology, reject that proposal; continue searching for a substantive content improvement if the improvement aim is still open, and stop only with a by-value no-proposal disposition.

Continue only when at least one predicted evaluation-result change states a scale-qualified change worth its cost and risk. Use `ExpectedEvaluationResultChange@Context` when the conditional account in §4.2a is needed. Switch method when the current method family is not changing the evaluated result, is too costly, or no longer fits the evaluation. Use `holdUntilInformationBasisSufficient` only with non-empty unfilled-position descriptions and the sufficiency condition that would make continuation admissible.

A needed observation can prepare that continuation without itself changing the object under improvement. Identify the unresolved question, how the observation could change the next decision, and what information would be enough. For example, §4.4.1 can return to current-process observation before selecting an intervention. Keep that preparatory result distinct from an improvement pass and from evidence that quality improved.

An all-`5`, all-exceptional, current-front-reaching, or current-front-improving result is neither necessary nor sufficient to stop. Use the practical gain, protected trade-offs and attainable cost in the preceding rule. A stopped loop can reopen when a new use, `Q` component, source anchor, `SoTA` front, comparison set, affordability boundary or worthwhile proposal changes that judgement. If the object still fails its required use and no repair is feasible, choose replacement, withdrawal or another admissible continuation; stopping work does not make that object adequate.

Treat the five decision values as current continuation dispositions, not as Work states. When the admitted A.22 structure is used, a branch is usable only when its guarded continuation cites the exact current guard or constraint claim and the already-obtaining relation occurrences that make that alternative admissible. A stop or subject-assertion reconsideration is a boundary until an exact stronger predicate and current facts establish another relation. Naming A.15, E.22, G.11, G.5, or another subject pattern as a locator neither performs Work nor creates an object described there.

##### E.23:4.4.1 - Improve a work process through target conditions and experiments

Use this branch when a team must improve an actual process but cannot responsibly prescribe the whole path to the desired state. The working question is: **What should the process do next, what currently prevents that, and which observation or experiment can justify the next move?** Rother's Improvement Kata provides a way to answer it repeatedly. A useful local result can be a bounded target condition and an informative next step; it need not be a complete solution to the longer-term challenge.

Choose an observable process and an attainable period of work. When people are learning the way, a small process with frequent, visible repetitions makes the connection between action and consequence easier to examine. Later applications can address harder or slower work. In a long-cycle process, an offline trial or simulation can provide earlier information, while the conditions that only real operation can establish remain to be checked there. Use an already adequate way directly when the receiving task needs no inquiry or practice.

**Establish direction and study the current process.** State the challenge that makes this improvement worthwhile, the desired outcome and the qualities that must be protected. A direction may come from a larger organizational target, but the team still needs to understand its own process. A long-term direction is not a predetermined list of intermediate solutions.

Observe where the work happens. Follow the material, information or customer request through the relevant steps; identify how work arrives, how an output is completed and where waiting, rework or variation occurs. Compare recent outcome observations with the required performance. Then examine the operating pattern that could explain that difference. For repeated output, observations at a consistent completion point distinguish the interval between outputs from the time spent on an individual task. Retain unusual observations and the circumstances that may explain them. An average can conceal the interruption or variant that should change the next action.

Use the analysis that the process requires. Where demand and available time govern output, derive the needed rate from those values. Where equipment is involved, distinguish unattended running, handling that delays the next cycle, and work that can occur in parallel. Where staffing capacity is the question, examine repeatable work content and variation before treating a simplified capacity estimate as available capacity. For office or project work, trace actual cases, their information dependencies and delays. These distinctions select a useful next question; they do not impose one manufacturing worksheet on every process. Initial observations can be sufficient to choose a step even while other facts remain unknown. Refresh them after intervening changes.

**Construct the next target condition.** Combine a near achieve-by date, the outcome sought within that scope, and the operating attributes expected to produce it. For example, a target might concern how complete inputs become available, how often an output is completed, or how work passes between two operations. Keep safety, correctness and other protected qualities explicit. The target describes a desired operating state; buying software or instructing operators describes a possible means of reaching it.

Derive this target from the current observations and the direction. Choose enough stretch to require useful learning, with a horizon and scope in which the team can act and see consequences. A wider ambition may first require a smaller target. Keep the operating attributes already adequate and leave genuinely unknown details unresolved rather than filling a form with invented knowledge. When a missing fact prevents even a meaningful target, obtain that fact first. The next target after this one will depend on what is learned; it need not be fixed now.

**Select an obstacle and the boundary of present knowledge.** Ask what observed condition prevents this target. A current queue, an inconsistent input or an unexplained variation can be an obstacle. “We have not installed our preferred solution” has not yet identified one. Describe what that solution would change and check whether the corresponding problem is present. An acknowledged lack of knowledge can itself determine the first step.

Keep a provisional list when several possible impediments would otherwise be lost, and select one relevant, manageable obstacle for the next investigation. The list is not a commitment to remove every item. Later observations can show that an item was mistaken, has disappeared or no longer matters to the target. A beginner may learn from a tractable obstacle; selecting the largest one is not a universal prerequisite.

**Choose the next step from what is not yet known.** Three useful choices have different results:

- **Observe the process without changing it** when the current condition or the supposed obstacle is insufficiently known. State what the observation should distinguish, where to look and what facts to bring back. An expectation about what will be seen helps expose assumptions; it need not be a fabricated numerical effect prediction.
- **Try a bounded exploratory change** when a small, permissible perturbation can reveal how the process responds. Predict the response as far as the present knowledge permits and identify what remains uncertain. Protect the people, product and continuing work if the prediction fails.
- **Test a particular explanation** when the proposed causal relationship is clear enough to discriminate. State the predicted effect, the observations and comparison that could support or challenge it, and the conditions needed for that inference.

**Match the comparison to the claim.** To learn how a component affects performance, arrange variation that distinguishes its contribution from other changes. When components may interact, compare the relevant combinations: the effect of one change may depend on whether another is present. A qualified factorial design can support those distinctions. When the decision concerns adopting a complete new way of working, compare that way with the available alternative under comparable receiving conditions; the warranted conclusion concerns their overall difference. A comparison of whole ways does not by itself identify their components' separate effects. The subject's experimental method determines allocation, replication, measurement and analysis. Carry changes in inputs, assistance or operating conditions into that design or restrict the resulting claim.

**Choose the size and timing of the next contribution.** Select the least burdensome sufficient action or coordinated set of actions for the decision, including observation, analysis or preparation where needed. One obstacle may require several factors or several experiments to be considered together. When the experimental arrangement remains unsettled, compare feasible alternatives, such as a single trial, a planned batch or an adaptive sequence, by the information they can supply, their full preparation and execution burden, delay and risk. With long feedback, a batch can examine useful combinations in one waiting period; an adaptive sequence can justify additional waiting when an earlier result can usefully change a later experiment. A planned sequence may also need preparation whose value appears only in the later comparison. C.40:4.7 supplies the related choice of how to search and compare candidate ways; the relevant domain method supplies the experiment itself.

Before acting, agree the intended action or set, the expected result or learning, how it will be observed, the limits on harm and the point at which useful information should be available. A short trial is useful only if the process has had time and opportunity to produce the relevant observation. Involve the people who perform and understand the work. A coach can help the learner design this contribution while leaving the learner's needed judgement observable.

**Compare and decide what follows.** After the step, distinguish the observed facts from the explanation drawn from them. Compare them with the expectation stated before acting. The result may support the explanation, contradict it, partly fit it or leave the question unresolved. Use the difference to update the current condition and choose what now needs to be learned or changed. A successful prediction can also provide useful knowledge. Rother uses experimentation broadly for this learning sequence; an observation or exploratory trial does not acquire the warrant of a controlled causal test by that name.

Retain a useful change only within the conditions its evidence supports. Before sustained use, check the protected qualities, the affected process as a whole and the operating conditions under which the change must continue to work. A successful trial and a stable working arrangement are different results. Return to §4.3 for the justified improvement continuation or stop; observation undertaken to fill a missing basis retains its preparatory status. Keep an adequate current arrangement when another change has no worthwhile contribution.

When the target is achieved or its agreed date arrives, compare the result with the original target and review what the process and the participants have learned. A missed target remains a missed target for that interval. Decide the next target from the resulting current condition and direction. If demand, safety, scope or the larger purpose has materially changed, explicitly reconsider the target and its grounds; do not preserve an obsolete target merely to keep a routine going or rewrite the old result as success. HCD.9:4.10 supplies the separate review of the learner's reasoning and the coach's help.

**Worked example — a contract-entry bottleneck.** This constructed example adapts the reasoning difficulty in the Practice Guide's sales case; its times and subsequent trials are illustrative, not measurements reported by Rother.

A team wants an ordinary contract completed within 20 minutes. Older observations suggest 31–39 minutes overall, including 10–14 minutes entering data. The learner proposes automating entry. The coach asks when those observations were made and what now consumes the entry time. Because the available observations predate a change to the input form, the next useful step is to observe current contracts, separate typing from lookup and correction, and bring back the sequence and times. The team does not yet have a justified automation proposal.

Current observations of the selected ordinary variant show 11–13 minutes of entry, including three to five minutes repeatedly searching for approved item codes. The team agrees a near target: by Friday, entry for this variant should take at most eight minutes, with verified inputs available when entry starts, no invalid codes and no added delay elsewhere. The 20-minute overall challenge remains open. The desired input condition does not prescribe whether its means should be software, a changed handover or another arrangement.

The learner now predicts that a view of the existing approved code source, available at entry, will remove repeated lookup without adding a separate preparation task. A safe offline comparison uses matched saved contracts, keeps their content and correctness checks comparable, and alternates which procedure is tried first. This helps distinguish the changed lookup procedure from simple familiarity with a particular contract. Both code correctness and time are observed. In the constructed result, entry takes seven to eight minutes and the codes remain correct for the tested variant. That is a reason to examine a bounded supervised application and the whole-process consequences. It is not proof that every contract can now be completed within 20 minutes. Representative live work and maintained access still determine whether the change can become the ordinary arrangement.

The same contract work can pose a different experimental question. Suppose the proposed route changes both the input handover and the code view. If the team needs to understand whether the changes work separately or together, it needs observations that distinguish the four combinations, with the assignment of contracts, repeated observations and analysis chosen to support that inference. If it only needs to choose between the complete new route and the current route, compare those two arrangements for correctness, completion time and total work. That second comparison can support the route choice without identifying which component caused the difference.

**A condition that changes the next move.** A special contract needs a customer choice before an item code can be approved. A faster lookup cannot supply that missing decision. If this contract type is now required, the team first establishes when and by whom the choice can be obtained, and revisits the input handover or target scope. It preserves the demonstrated benefit for the ordinary variant without claiming the broader target. If the customer response time makes Friday's broader target infeasible, that fact changes the target decision; it is not a reason to keep running identical lookup experiments.

The process and coaching sequence is developed from Mike Rother's *The Toyota Kata Practice Guide*, the 2018 electronic edition, Chapters 5–9 and 11–12. It supplies current-condition analysis, target-condition construction and learning at the boundary of knowledge. The experimental distinctions use the NIST/SEMATECH handbook's [interaction](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pri/section2/pri212.htm), [factorial-design](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pri/section3/pri3331.htm) and [randomized-comparison](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pri/section3/pri331.htm) explanations. Treating a complete way as the compared alternative is the application made here. [Huan, Jagalur and Marzouk, revised 2026, §§5 and 6.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16212v2) distinguish batch and adaptive experimental design and its modeling and computational demands; model-based experimental-design optimisation requires a suitable model and enough expected benefit to justify its computation. The contract examples are constructed applications. Rother supplies a practice prescription and teaching cases; the experimental-design sources justify comparisons under their stated model conditions.

#### E.23:4.5 - Operation-family selection

An operation family is selected only when the loop account names:

1. one scale-qualified predicted evaluation-result change;
2. failure mode addressed;
3. cost or risk reason;
4. protected trade-offs;
5. stop or removal condition.

Typical operation families are specification articulation, task decomposition, context refresh with carry-forward evidence, failure-context retry, verification against specification, memory or distillation, external critic or co-regulation, proposal portfolio use, search breadth or variants, bounded object-change budget, held-out evaluation, rejected-change memory, optimizer-memory separation, source-anchor contribution assignment, agent-tool-interface hardening, and task-family adaptation signature. They remain selectable only for the loop that justifies them.

#### E.23:4.6 - Cost and BLP discipline

`C.19.1` governs the preference for broad, scale-amenable methods when safety, admissibility, and practical fitness are comparable. `E.23` uses that preference but does not assume that accepted-work cost is one number. Compare material resources, tools and instruments, adaptation attempts, skilled attention, rework or delay, risk exposure, and avoided loss on their admitted scales. Keep the components separate, reject a dominated option, and use the declared project policy to choose or hold when no option dominates.

Net-cost arithmetic is permitted only after every term has been converted to one declared unit through an admissible conversion whose basis, uncertainty, and scope remain visible. Until then, avoided loss is a separate project estimate rather than a quantity subtracted from concrete burden. A justified avoided loss can still make an expensive loop preferable. For a simple object, a direct edit or adjustment, small repair, lower-cost performer, specialized cycle, or one-shot evaluation can remain the better option.

Choose harness improvement when a named defect in framing, tools, memory, verification or stopping causes avoidable retry and repairing it compares favourably with direct object repair or another available method. Apply `C.11.DUA` to preparation, recurring use, transition, independent judgement, maintenance and displaced useful work. Leave unavailable cost evidence unknown; a larger harness is not itself a gain.

#### E.23:4.7 - Source-composed, OEE, and NQD improvement

Accepted `SoTA` is the working external front only when assigned by the object-under-improvement evaluation, accepted source-use decision, or declared comparison set. `E.23` can govern a loop that reaches, maintains, or improves relative to that front; it does not self-assign `SoTA`.

When an evaluation-result change depends on source use, source currentness, or a dated external front, the loop record cites the exact accepted source contribution and bounded use, including a `G.2` pack result when that route was used, or the relevant `G.11` currentness result, with the edition or date needed for replay. `E.23` carries that reference; it does not make the source-use or currentness decision.

When several source anchors are used, retain each accepted source-use decision and contribution. If the improvement depends on their combined use, construct the needed connection under the subject Method; for framework Methods, use C.39 and E.4.CM. Preserve independently usable contributions when no composite Method is needed. Relate the changed result claim to those decisions and contributions and re-evaluate the changed object version. Use `SourceComposedResultClaim` within a separately constituted result episteme when the receiving use needs that exact account.

For NQD and OEE, use `E.23` to change one object version or candidate and re-evaluate it on declared `Q` coordinates. Use `C.17` for novelty, diversity, descriptors, and distances, `C.18` for archive and front insertion, `C.19` for pool policy, `G.5` for selected-set result declaration, `G.9` for parity, and `G.11` for currentness and refresh. When audience availability is current, use `E.17` for a source-backed publication face and return to source and `E.24.PUB` for the publication occurrence, form, carrier, audience, bounded use, and availability.
